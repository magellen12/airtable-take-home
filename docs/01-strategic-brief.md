# Strategic Brief — High Touch Customer Success
**Director, AI Transformation & Customer Success · First 90 days**

> The mandate is to move this function from a **relationship-led** motion to a
> **transformation-led** one. Everything below is in service of that shift, sequenced against
> the renewals that won't wait for it.

---

## What I see in the book

Six accounts, $5.06M ARR. **$3.11M of it — 61% — has a renewal conversation inside three
quarters**: Floor & Board, Voltaic, Corvus. All three score **1 out of 5 on Value evidence** —
whether anyone can state, in the buyer's language, what the customer got for the money.

I scored every account on four dimensions — **Adoption depth, Sponsorship & multi-threading,
Governance maturity, Value evidence** (full working in [`02-book-diagnosis.md`](02-book-diagnosis.md)):

| Account | ARR | Renewal | Adoption | Sponsor | Govern | **Value ev.** | Stage | Binding constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Floor & Board | $1.7M | ~2Q | 3 | 1 | 1 | **1** | 2 → regressing | Sponsorship (+ CoE) |
| Meridian Health | $1.1M | 4Q | 4 | 5 | 3 | **2** | 3 Governed | Value evidence |
| TrailLine | $540K | first yr | 1 | 3 | 1 | **1** | 1 Sponsored | Adoption |
| Corvus Financial | $890K | 3Q | 4 | 2 | 2 | **1** | 2 Contained | Value evidence |
| Harbor Lane | $310K | 5Q | 2 | 1 | 1 | **1** | 0 Unaware | Value evidence |
| Voltaic | $520K | 2Q | 5 | 2 | 1 | **2** | 2 *(presents as 4)* | Governance |
| **Book average** | | | **3.2** | **2.3** | **1.5** | **1.3** | | |

Adoption averages **3.2** while Value evidence averages **1.3**. In no account is the blocker
"Airtable can't do this." Every blocker is organizational: nobody owns the outcome, nobody
governs the build, nobody can prove the value.

**So the premise I'm running on: this function's job is organizational change management with a
platform attached, not feature adoption.** A relationship-led motion drives usage. A
transformation-led one produces owners, governance, and evidence — the three things this book
is starving for. Everything follows from that.

### Three structural patterns — and what's just account noise

**1. Value realization doesn't exist as a discipline. (6 of 6.)** Not one account has a
current, quantified business-outcome narrative in the language of the person who signs. And the
tell is in the split: where value **is** being created — Corvus (field agents in production),
Voltaic (self-built workflows) — we cannot speak to it; where it **isn't** — Floor & Board,
Harbor Lane — nobody noticed until Sales flagged risk. **The renewal risk is not an adoption
problem. It's an evidence problem**, and it's structural because no artifact, ritual, or role
currently produces the evidence.

**2. Transformation is person-dependent — on both sides of the table.** On the customer side,
it lives in one head: Floor & Board's roadmap died when its champion left; Corvus's adoption
sits behind a single BU champion; TrailLine is one enthusiastic COO over a hostile user layer.
On our side, account outcomes track individual CSM strengths rather than a common motion — our
strongest builder is walking into TrailLine's executive dynamics, and our best relationship
manager can't quantify Corvus's story. **Anything that lives in one person's head is a standing
risk on a multi-year contract. The function's job is to institutionalize** — and with the
manager layer gone, we currently have no inspection surface to institutionalize *anything*,
which caps how fast every other fix can move.

**3. We engage without a readiness model.** TrailLine got an executive vision sale before
frontline buy-in existed. Harbor Lane got nothing, because nobody has a play for "healthy but
dormant." Voltaic outgrew quarterly check-ins two quarters ago and we kept scheduling them.
Meridian is *ready* — sponsor, CoE, appetite — and blocked at governance, and we brought no
governance case. Today, when and how AI gets introduced depends on the CSM's comfort, not the
customer's readiness.

**What's genuinely account-specific** — handled as work, not as an input to methodology: Floor
& Board's cross-team automation break (Support owns it), Meridian's particular review board,
TrailLine's kickoff date.

### The trap in the book

**Voltaic looks like the most advanced account and is the most dangerous.** Self-building
agents, growing seats, technically confident — it presents as Stage 4. But the build is
ungoverned by the customer's own admission, CS has no seat, QBR attendance has fallen two
quarters running, and it renews in **two quarters** into a pricing negotiation we won't be in
the room for. Seat growth without CS influence isn't health; it's a customer that has priced us
as a tool. I'd read any book for this shape.

## My top three priorities

**P1 · Stand up the transformation methodology — diagnostic → stage → plays. (Days 1–30.)**
Every account gets staged on one shared readiness diagnostic across the four dimensions, by its
CSM, in the Airtable base (deliverable #2). Stage determines the play from a defined library,
each play with a clear *CSM-owns-vs-partners* boundary. I start here because with no manager
layer I have no shared language and no inspection surface, and I can't coach what I can't see —
and the diagnostic does double duty: **how a CSM scores and defends their account tells me more
about their depth in 45 minutes than a month of skip-levels.** One book baseline and one team
assessment from the same exercise.

**Governance/CoE work is a required play in this library, not an aspiration** — because it's the
single answer to three different accounts: Meridian (co-build the security case that unblocks
the review board), Voltaic (ungoverned agents are our wedge back to relevance — CS as the
governance partner, not the check-in scheduler), and Floor & Board (a CoE is what makes the
vision survive the *next* champion's departure).

**P2 · Build the value-realization engine, pointed at the next three quarters of renewals.
(Days 15–60.)** *Baseline → instrument → quantify → narrate.* For every account renewing inside
three quarters, we define two or three business outcomes *in the customer's language*, instrument
the signals behind them, and produce a renewal-grade value narrative before commercial
conversations start. The same asset powers expansion: Corvus's CFO and procurement won't fund an
enterprise rollout on a BU's enthusiasm, but they will on quantified cycle-time and cost numbers.
**Retention and expansion stop being hope and become a pipeline of evidence.**

**P3 · Rebuild the team's operating system — roles, rhythms, development. (Days 30–90.)**
Fifteen people who just lost their manager layer don't need a new boss with a slide deck; they
need clarity and a system that develops them. Concretely: a **skills matrix** across the book
(builder depth × executive presence × domain); deliberate **pairing of complements** — TrailLine's
builder-CSM gets an exec-engagement partner for the kickoff rather than a reassignment; a weekly
portfolio review run *from the operating base, not from decks*; and enablement built directly
from the play library. This is the answer to fifteen CSMs of varied depth: I don't need every
CSM credible with a CFO on AI risk — I need every CSM to diagnose that the account needs that
conversation and to pull the right partner in.

**What I'm deliberately not doing in the first 90 days**, and why:
- **Not hiring the manager layer.** I want to see the team run the system first; leads should
  surface through play authorship and diagnostic quality, not tenure. I'd make that call at day
  90 with evidence.
- **Not re-cutting segmentation or coverage.** The intuitive move with a book this uneven, and a
  six-month distraction from three renewals I have to survive first.
- **Not building a formal enablement curriculum.** Enablement rides on the plays — CSMs learn by
  running them on live accounts, not in a classroom.

## The methodology, and how it connects to money

**Stage *describes* where an account is; the binding constraint *prescribes* the next play.**
Full detail in [`03-transformation-methodology.md`](03-transformation-methodology.md). Two
mechanics make it work when a mid-tenure CSM runs it:

**The constraint is the lowest dimension, not the average.** An account's next move is set by its
weakest score, not its overall maturity. Meridian averages 3.5 and is completely stuck — because
its value evidence is a 2 and a review board is the gate, so Sponsorship at 5 and Adoption at 4
don't help. Corvus averages 2.25 and is one artifact away from an expansion. This is why the same
diagnostic produces sound recommendations for a CSM who doesn't yet have the pattern library in
their head.

**Below Stage 1, Value evidence leads; at Stage 1 and above, Sponsorship leads.** I needed this
second rule after the model gave a wrong answer on Harbor Lane — it wanted me to recruit an
executive sponsor for something nobody there has seen yet. You can't sponsor an abstraction. At
Stage 0 you prove one narrow thing first, and *then* you have something to sponsor.

**The commercial linkage is the value narrative, and it's a dated artifact, not a conversation.**
Signals → quantified outcome → narrative in the buyer's language → validated by the customer →
carried into renewal and expansion. Renewals are lost in the gap between "they use it a lot" and
"here's what it was worth." Expansion is won by closing that gap early enough that procurement
argues about scope rather than value. The base enforces the dating: a value narrative older than
one quarter on an account renewing within two is flagged as a risk in the same view as a churn
signal, because it is one.

## Operationalizing it across 15 CSMs

**The ownership rule:** a CSM must always own being the person who knows what their account
needs. **A CSM must never have to be the smartest technical person in the room.**

| CSM always owns | Partner leads, CSM orchestrates |
|---|---|
| The diagnostic and its evidence | AI solution architecture (Professional Services / Solutions) |
| The stakeholder and sponsor map | Governance, risk and security artifacts (Trust + Product) |
| The value narrative and the executive story | Commercial construct and renewal path (Sales + Renewals) |
| The customer's operating rhythm and transformation plan | Break/fix and technical escalation (Support) |

The **skills matrix and deliberate pairing** are what make varied depth a staffing question
instead of a performance problem — detail in [`04-operating-model.md`](04-operating-model.md).
TrailLine's builder-CSM owns the ops-floor re-contract (their strength) and is *paired* with an
exec-engagement partner for the COO thread, rather than reassigned. Corvus's relationship-strong
CSM keeps the CFO's office (their strength) while the base drafts the numbers and Professional
Services validates them. Neither is asked to become someone else.

**Inspection, run weekly out of the base — not out of slides:**

*Leading (I own these):* % of book staged; **stage progression QoQ** (the maturity-progression
metric); sponsor coverage (named exec + ≥3 threads — today 1 of 6); **value coverage** (a
customer-validated narrative ready ≥1 quarter before renewal — today 0 of 6); governance body in
place; play cycle time; partner pull-through.
*Lagging:* GRR, NRR, CS-sourced expansion pipeline, and **renewal surprises, target zero** — a
surprise at renewal is a diagnostic failure 6 months earlier, and it's the metric I'd be judged
on. The four-dimension diagnostic *is* our customer-health instrument — multidimensional, so it
never averages away the one score that predicts a loss.

*Rhythm:* Monday 60-minute book review in the base, three accounts deep and rotating. Biweekly
1:1s that walk one account against its diagnostic. Monthly play retro where CSMs — not I — retire
and author plays. Quarterly re-baseline plus a customer-facing value review. I report to
leadership at day 45 and day 90 out of the same base I inspect the team in. No parallel deck
economy.

## How the build carries this

A methodology that lives in a doc dies the way Floor & Board's backlog did. The Airtable
prototype ([`airtable-build/`](../airtable-build/)) is the methodology made operational — it
implements P1 and P2. Accounts carry their diagnostic scores and live signals; an AI field
classifies the stage and drafts the rationale; a second recommends the play from the library; a
third turns adoption signals into a first draft of the value narrative in the buyer's language. An
automation flags any account inside two quarters of renewal whose narrative is stale or missing.
The Director view is the whole book on one screen: stage distribution, value coverage, ARR at
risk by constraint, plays overdue.

That's the leverage test — and it's also the inspection layer. The newest CSM's account gets the
same diagnostic rigor and a *drafted* transformation plan as the strongest CSM's; senior judgment
goes into refining and running plays, not formatting updates. The portfolio review and my own view
of the book run from the same screens the team works in.

**The honest caveat:** the AI drafts, the human validates. A value narrative is not real until the
customer says the number back to us, and the base tracks `Draft → Reviewed → Customer-validated`
precisely so we never walk an AI-generated number into a CFO conversation unchecked.
